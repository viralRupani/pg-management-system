How to Reduce Vacant Beds: A PG Occupancy Playbook

A vacant bed isn't a neutral zero — it's a loss, every single day it stays empty. Here's a practical playbook for closing that gap without racing to the bottom on rent.

B Basera Team 18 Aug 2026 6 min read

Occupancy is the one number that decides whether a PG is profitable, break-even, or quietly losing money — more than rent price, more than amenities. A well-run 100-bed PG at 95% occupancy will out-earn a nicer 100-bed PG at 75%, every time. Yet most owners track occupancy in their head, roughly, rather than as a number they check weekly.

Know your number before you try to fix it

You can't shorten a gap you're not measuring. Start with a simple weekly check: total beds, occupied beds, and — the number that actually predicts next month's vacancy — beds with a resident who has given notice or is likely to leave soon. Most PGs only see a vacancy coming the week it happens. Tracking upcoming exits gives you a two-to-four week head start to fill the bed before it goes empty.

1. Start marketing a bed before it's empty

If a resident tells you they're moving out at month-end, that bed should go "available from" on your listings the same day — not the day they actually leave. A bed that's pre-booked to move in the day after the old resident leaves has zero vacancy days. A bed you only start advertising after it's empty has however many days it takes you to find someone, multiplied by every month you repeat the same delay.

2. Fix your response time, not just your listing

Enquiries for shared accommodation move fast — a prospective resident is usually comparing 3-4 PGs the same day. If your response to an enquiry takes till evening because you were busy running the property, you've likely lost that lead to whoever replied first. A fast, consistent response (even a simple "yes available, can you visit today?") converts far better than a perfect listing with a slow reply.

3. Make the visit-to-booking step frictionless

By the time someone visits in person, they've already decided the location and price work. What they're deciding on the visit is trust — is this run properly, or does it feel chaotic. A clean bed map you can show them, a clear written breakdown of rent + deposit + what's included, and a same-day booking process (not "come back with your documents next week") all shorten the gap between visit and moved-in.

4. Track why beds go empty, not just that they did

Every exit has a reason — end of course/job, price, a complaint that went unresolved, a room conflict. If you're not recording it, you can't tell the difference between "normal churn" and "a fixable pattern." A PG that's consistently losing residents in month 3 for the same complaint has an operations problem, not a marketing one, and no amount of faster listing turnaround fixes that.

5. Use booked-ahead beds to protect your pipeline

When you know a bed will free up in three weeks, hold it as reserved for an incoming resident rather than leaving it fully open until the day it's vacated. This is exactly the gap between a bed showing as "occupied" right up to move-out and then suddenly "vacant," versus a bed that transitions straight from one resident to the next with a booking already in place.

The PGs with the highest occupancy aren't the cheapest ones — they're the ones where a bed spends the fewest days doing nothing.

6. Don't discount your way to full — fix the gap instead

Slashing rent to fill a stubborn vacancy fast feels like progress, but it resets the price expectation for every future resident in that room, including the ones already paying full rent who'll eventually notice. It's almost always cheaper to fix the actual reason the bed is sitting empty — slow response, bad listing photos, an unresolved complaint driving churn — than to discount permanently against a temporary problem.

The weekly check worth doing Every week: how many beds are vacant right now, how many will free up in the next 30 days, and how many enquiries came in versus how many converted to a visit. Three numbers, five minutes, and you'll catch a vacancy problem while it's still small.

FAQ: PG occupancy

What's a good occupancy rate for a PG?

There's no universal number — it depends on your city and segment — but the goal isn't a target percentage, it's minimizing the days any individual bed sits empty between residents.

Should I lower rent to fill a vacant bed faster?

Only as a last resort, and only for that specific bed if there's a real reason it's hard to fill (a lower floor, no window). A blanket discount to fill vacancy usually signals a deeper issue worth fixing instead.

How far in advance should I start marketing a bed?

As soon as you know it's coming free — ideally the same day a resident gives notice, not the day they actually leave.
