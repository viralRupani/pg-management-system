Security Deposits for PG Owners: Rules, Deductions & Avoiding Disputes

The deposit is the one part of a resident relationship where trust is highest at move-in and lowest at move-out. Here's how to keep it that way at both ends.

B Basera Team 1 Sep 2026 7 min read

This isn't legal advice — specific deposit rules and caps can vary by state, and some cities apply general tenancy regulations to PG accommodation too, so it's worth checking what applies locally, especially for larger deposit amounts. What follows is the operational practice that keeps a deposit dispute from happening in the first place, regardless of the exact local rule.

Setting the deposit amount

Most PGs set a deposit equal to one or two months' rent — enough to cover a genuine loss (unpaid final month, real damage) without being so large it becomes a barrier to moving in, or a number residents feel is being held hostage. A deposit that's disproportionate to your actual rent invites more disputes than it prevents, because residents start to view getting it back as unlikely rather than routine.

Collecting it: all at once, or in installments

Not every resident can pay a full deposit plus first month's rent on day one — accepting a partial deposit at booking, topped up at move-in, is a reasonable way to hold a bed for someone without demanding the full amount upfront. Just be precise about what's been collected so far versus what's still owed; a deposit tracked as a single lump figure makes partial payments easy to lose track of.

What's reasonable to deduct at move-out

  • Unpaid rent for the final month or any prior pending dues — the most common and least disputed deduction.
  • Genuine damage beyond normal wear — a broken fixture, damaged furniture — ideally documented with a photo at move-out, compared against the move-in condition.
  • Missing inventory — if you provide furnishings (mattress, cupboard, study table) and something's missing, deduct its reasonable replacement cost, not an inflated one.

What's usually not reasonable

  • Normal wear — scuffed paint, a worn mattress after a year of use — isn't damage.
  • A blanket "cleaning charge" applied regardless of the room's actual condition.
  • Deducting for something that was never documented as provided or as damaged.
The deposit dispute that turns into a bad review almost never starts with the amount — it starts with a deduction the resident was never shown evidence for.

The paperwork that prevents a dispute

Two documents do almost all the work: a simple move-in condition note (what furnishings were provided, their state) and a final settlement statement at move-out that itemises every deduction against the deposit, with the net refund clearly shown. Neither needs to be elaborate — it needs to exist, and the resident needs to see it before the money moves, not after.

Mid-tenancy refunds

Deposits aren't always a single collect-then-refund event. A resident downgrading to a cheaper room, or a partial deposit being adjusted for a documented reason, both need the deposit balance to reflect reality at any point in time — not just at final move-out. If your records only show "deposit: ₹X, collected" as one static figure, a mid-tenancy adjustment has nowhere to go except a side note you'll have to remember later.

Settling the exit cleanly

When a resident's approved move-out date arrives, the deposit — minus any deductions — should be returned promptly, and the final invoice reconciled against it (a deposit can often cover an outstanding final month's rent rather than requiring a separate payment). A clean, itemised settlement at the end is also the best goodwill a PG can leave a departing resident with — it's frequently what shows up in reviews, for better or worse.

A simple exit checklist Confirm the move-out date → inspect the room against the move-in note → itemise any deductions → apply the remaining deposit to any pending rent → refund the balance and share the statement, same day if possible.

FAQ: PG security deposits

How much deposit should I charge?

One to two months' rent is typical for most PGs — enough to cover a real loss without being a barrier to moving in. Check any local caps that may apply to your city.

Can I deduct for normal wear and tear?

Generally no — normal wear from ordinary use isn't the same as damage, and deducting for it is one of the most common sources of a deposit dispute.

What if a resident leaves without giving notice?

Your policy on this should be set and communicated at move-in (e.g. forfeiting part of the deposit in lieu of notice), documented in whatever move-in agreement you use, so it isn't a surprise applied after the fact.
